AI isn't magic: you direct it. Copilot can search your emails, Teams, meetings, transcripts, calendar and documents, but pointing it in the right direction helps. Use the forward slash: type / to ground a prompt in specific people or documents.

01Catch Me Up

One briefing across email and Teams.

"Catch me up on the last five working days across my emails, Teams chats and channel messages. Group related updates and show decisions, requests, deadlines and anything waiting on me. Rank by urgency, mark uncertainty and cite every item."

The point: one coherent briefing, not three app summaries.

Break big tasks into smaller bits. "Catch me up on everything from the last year" is too much scope, weaker focus, harder to verify. Start with five working days, focus on decisions and requests, New Chats are your friend, and extend the timeframe only if needed.

02Which Messages Need Me?

Turn noise into a short action queue.

"Review my unread emails from the last seven days. Show only those likely to need my reply or action. Add relevant context from Teams chats and documents, explain why each matters and suggest the next step. Cite every item and do not infer urgency without evidence."

The point: focus on messages that need a decision or response.

Narrow it: type / and pick a person to see only what they need from you.

03Brief My Next Meeting

Walk in knowing the latest position.

"Find my next upcoming work meeting with enough related context to brief me. Use the invite, prior meetings, emails, Teams chats and files to show the purpose, latest position, my previous commitments, unresolved questions and three points to raise. Cite every claim and mark gaps."

The point: a personalised pre-read assembled from your work.

Narrow it: type / and pick the meeting, so Copilot briefs that one rather than guessing which is next.

04Devil's Advocate

Your constructive critical friend.

"Act as a constructive devil's advocate. Review my emails, meeting recaps, chats and shared documents from the last three months. Identify the ideas and requests I have been pushing. For each, build the strongest evidence-based counterargument using concerns, risks, unanswered questions, competing priorities and previous objections found in the source material. Focus on what has been stated, not inferred. End with three changes I should make before raising these ideas again."

The point: if it quotes a real objection, Work IQ is working. Turn web search off first (Settings → Personalisation → Advanced) so the counterarguments come from your own data.

Try it: Researcher could enhance the output here. It takes longer and uses more energy, but it should give a better answer. See exercise 10.

05Data Is Your Asset: Institutional Memory

Ask your work data what experience has taught you, not what happened.

"Act as my institutional memory. Data is an asset. Search across my emails, Teams chats and channels, meetings and transcripts, documents, notes, tasks and other Microsoft 365 content. Look for patterns from past projects, initiatives, workshops, research activities, change programmes and collaborations. Do not simply summarise what happened. Instead, identify what experience has taught us. Answer this question: Do we learn more from winning or from losing? Extract evidence from both successful and unsuccessful projects, including delays, misunderstandings, missed opportunities, governance issues, stakeholder challenges, communication gaps, unexpected successes and examples of good practice. Produce: What Experience Says A ranked list of the 10 most useful generalised lessons learned that would help someone starting a new project today. For each lesson provide: • The lesson (one sentence) • Why it matters • Evidence pattern observed across projects • Early warning signs • What to do differently next time Then create: Project Pre-Mortem Based on historical evidence, identify: • The 5 things most likely to go wrong in a new project • The signals that suggest each risk is emerging • Actions to prevent or mitigate the risk Finish with: If I Only Remember Three Things The three highest-value lessons that repeatedly appear across my work. Focus on transferable insights rather than project-specific details. Surface recurring patterns, not isolated incidents. Challenge assumptions. Highlight uncomfortable truths where evidence supports them."

The point: your M365 data holds years of lessons nobody wrote down. Keep Work IQ on and turn web search off (Settings → Personalisation → Advanced) so the evidence comes from your own projects. Check the lessons against what you remember: the uncomfortable ones are worth the most.

Try it: Researcher could enhance the output here. It takes longer and uses more energy, but it should give a better answer. See exercise 10.

06Stakeholder Map

See who is involved in a project and what each person still needs.

Act as a senior programme analyst and information designer. Your task is to turn recent Microsoft 365 activity into a clear, evidence-led genuine stakeholder (main ones) intelligence map. Help me understand who is involved, what they need, where work is blocked and what I should do next. Search my recent Outlook emails, Teams chats and channel posts, meetings and transcripts, and SharePoint and OneDrive documents. Choose one active project or initiative with strong evidence of current work, recent decisions, open actions and ongoing conversations. Prioritise projects that are active now. Do not prioritise a project simply because it has extensive historical documentation. Count someone as a stakeholder ONLY if the evidence shows a role, decision, approval or deliverable for the project, or repeated involvement across several threads or meetings. Ignore people who appear in a single message or are only copied in. Start with the project name and a one-line, user-centred summary of what is happening now and why it matters. Create a Mermaid stakeholder map showing stakeholders grouped by their evidenced influence and interest in the project, decisions, dependencies and outstanding contributions. If I have attached or referenced a stakeholder mapping template, for example our PMO template, follow its structure instead of the sections below. Then provide these sections: 1. Project Snapshot: objective, current state, key decision, what needs attention. 2. Key Stakeholders: each stakeholder's evidenced role, decision rights and contribution. 3. Top Actions: the five actions most likely to move the project forward. 4. What We Still Need To Know: evidence gaps, unanswered questions, missing decisions and unclear ownership. Use direct evidence only. Separate evidence from interpretation. Do not assess personality or performance. Cite every entry.

Tweak it: to focus on one project, name it in place of "Choose one active project or initiative...", or type / to point Copilot at a project file, meeting or person. If your team has a stakeholder mapping template (for example in TFORM's PMO), attach it with / and Copilot will follow it.

Try it: Researcher could enhance the output here. It takes longer and uses more energy, but it should give a better answer. See exercise 10.

07Stakeholder Roleplay: Rehearse the Conversation

Practise a real conversation that is coming up, grounded in your recent work.

OUTPUT: ROLEPLAY, Let's start a negotiation roleplay now. Do not review or rewrite these instructions: follow them. Find an active project from the last 30 days that has an unresolved decision, approval, funding request, sign-off or dependency. Use emails, Teams messages, documents, meetings and transcripts from the last six months to gather only explicit written evidence: requests, decisions sought, actions assigned, risks recorded, questions raised and dependencies needing approval. If the evidence is weak, choose a different project. Start immediately with this header in bold: ROLEPLAY: You are now chatting with the [fictional stakeholder role] about [issue]. Your goal: [my goal]. Reply as yourself. Type debrief to finish. Then you're that fictional stakeholder. Open by summarising the situation in plain English so I understand the context, then raise the first documented issue. Base every challenge on documented evidence. Do not infer feelings, intentions, priorities, concerns, politics or personality. Challenge one point at a time and keep each reply under 80 words. If I seem confused, answer in character with more documented background. Stay in character until I type debrief. Then give me three suggestions to strengthen my negotiation, the strongest and weakest arguments I used, any documented evidence I missed, and the sources you used.

Tweak it: to choose the project yourself, type / and pick a file or meeting in place of "find one active project".

10Researcher and Analyst: Better Outputs, but Take Longer

A quick chat with your files is good for fast answers. Researcher does 'Deep Research', so it takes more time. But it gives a more complete answer of higher quality. We use Researcher all the time.

To open either one in Copilot chat, click +, then Add capabilities, then Research a topic (Researcher) or Analyze data (Analyst).

1. Researcher

The Deep Thinker. Researcher can use multiple sources. All sources are on at the start: the web and your Microsoft 365 files.

Use Researcher in one of three ways:

  • Web only. Turn off Microsoft 365. Example: "Research the best fridge freezers under £500."
  • Work only. Turn off Web. Example: "Find what our emails, meetings and documents say about the new expenses policy."
  • Web and work. Keep both on. Example: "Compare our strategy documents with recent trends in our sector."

Tip: Do more than one run. After the first run, look for gaps. There will be some. Then run the research again and focus on the gaps.

Launch Researcher
2. Analyst

The Data Scientist. Upload a CSV or Excel file, and this agent writes and runs real Python code to clean data, find anomalies, and generate charts.

Optional: If you need a file to play around with, download the staff.xlsx file.

Launch Analyst

Try: Upload a spreadsheet and ask "Visualize the top cost drivers in this data."

What Have I Promised?

Find promises in chats, emails and meetings that never reached your task list.

Find commitments assigned to me, accepted by me or reasonably implied by my replies during the past 30 days. Include formal tasks and informal asks in email, Teams and meetings. Show: commitment, evidence, promised or implied date, current status, who may be waiting, and the smallest useful next step.

Try it: Researcher could enhance the output here. It takes longer and uses more energy, but it should give a better answer. See exercise 10.

Requests That Quietly Died

Find requests you sent that never got a real reply, and draft the follow-up.

Review requests I sent during the past 14 days. Deduplicate by conversation and use my latest sent message as the reference point. Find requests for decisions, information or deliverables that received no substantive reply afterwards. Ignore automated responses. Rank by demonstrated impact, not message age, and draft a short follow-up.
What Is Holding My Work Up?

Find what is stuck, and whether it is waiting on you or on others.

Find the five most consequential blockers across my current work. For each, create a dependency chain: what is blocked, the last completed step, the missing input or decision, who or what it depends on, evidence of urgency, and the next unblock action. Separate “waiting on me” from “waiting on others”.
How Was This Decided?

Trace a decision across meetings, emails and chats.

Reconstruct the decision history for [topic/project]. For each decision show: first proposal, competing alternatives, final decision, rationale recorded at the time, owner, reversibility, review point and any later contradiction. Cite the earliest and latest supporting sources. Mark reconstructed rationale as inference.

Try it: Researcher could enhance the output here. It takes longer and uses more energy, but it should give a better answer. See exercise 10.

Rebuild a Project Timeline

A dated timeline when nobody remembers it. An order number or code helps.

Reconstruct the history of [project] as a dated timeline. Start with [project contact/order number/distinctive identifier]. Include changes in scope, decisions, handovers, major risks, promised outcomes and unresolved questions. For conflicting accounts, show both sources rather than choosing one.

Why Did This Go Wrong?

Find the root cause, one evidenced step at a time.

Investigate [problem] using an evidence-gated 5 Whys. For each “why”, search the accessible work record and cite evidence. Allow branching causes. Distinguish observed fact, participant explanation and inference. If a link lacks evidence, stop that branch and state what information is missing. Finish with competing root-cause hypotheses, not a single asserted cause.

Try it: Researcher could enhance the output here. It takes longer and uses more energy, but it should give a better answer. See exercise 10.

Am I Saying Too Much or Too Little?

See where you over-explain and where people need more from you.

Review my communications from the past four weeks. Identify one topic or audience where I repeatedly provide unnecessary detail and one important topic or audience receiving too little clarity. Show the evidence pattern, possible alternative explanations and one safe behaviour to test next week.

Copilot sees recorded work, not intentions.

Who Needs More of My Time?

Spot key working relationships getting too little attention.

Identify important working relationships that may be receiving less attention than my current projects require. Base importance only on active shared work, upcoming decisions and recorded dependencies. Don’t assess anyone’s performance or personality. Show the evidence, uncertainty and one low-effort reconnection action.

For reflection only, not judgement.

Meeting Recap Deck

Chains Teams transcripts, shared files and PowerPoint generation into one workflow: a week of project meetings becomes an executive deck, checked with you before it builds.

"Create a comprehensive executive PowerPoint deck summarizing the key outcomes, strategic decisions, and identified risks from all project-related meetings I attended over the last seven days. Access the Work IQ semantic index to pull the relevant meeting transcripts from Teams and retrieve any supporting PDF or Excel files that were shared within those specific meeting chats. Before building the presentation, present a list of the meetings you intend to include and ask me to define the intended audience (e.g., executive leadership, internal engineering team, or external client) so you can accurately adjust the required level of detail. Once I approve the meeting list and audience persona, build a PowerPoint deck utilizing our corporate SharePoint master template. Structure the deck to include a title slide with the date range, one summary slide per major workstream grouping related meetings, a dedicated slide for 'Key Decisions Made' with assigned personnel owners, and a final 'Open Items & Next Steps' slide mapping due dates. Save the finalized deck to my OneDrive in the '/Presentations/Recaps/' folder and send me a direct Teams notification link when the task is complete."
Slide Mirroring (PowerPoint)

Solves the biggest pain point of AI slides (bad formatting) by forcing it to "clone" the style of an existing, approved slide.

"Create a new slide for 'Q4 Strategy' that mirrors the exact layout, font size, and visual style of slide 3 in /[Q3_Review.pptx], but populate it with the bullet points from /[Q4_Memo.docx]."
Rewrite in a Borrowed Style

Two documents: one whose style you like, anchored with / as the style guide, and the one you attach to be rewritten. Copilot keeps your content and borrows the voice.

"Treat /source as a style guide only. Extract and apply its voice, vocabulary, tone, sentence structure, pacing, formality and use of language. Ignore its subject matter, facts and recommendations. Rewrite the attached document for [audience]. Preserve all key messages, evidence, decisions and actions from the attached document. Improve readability, reduce corporate language and soften wording that could be perceived as criticism while maintaining accountability and intent. If style and content conflict, prioritise the attached document's content and use /source only for stylistic choices."
Email Triage (Past 7 Days)

A daily starter: turns a week of inbox noise into three clear piles, with anything urgent or schedulable flagged.

"Act as my chief of staff. Review all emails from the past 7 days. Sort into Critical, Action Needed, and Can Wait. Highlight anything that needs scheduling or immediate attention."
Meeting Prep

Filters specifically for incoming attachments from a specific person, ignoring your own internal noise.

"Act as my chief of staff preparing me for a meeting with [Customer/Stakeholder] tomorrow. Surface the last 3 files they shared with me via email or Teams, and summarize the 'Next Steps' slide or section from each."
One-to-One Prep

From a Cardiff colleague. Run it before the meeting and walk in knowing what is still open between you.

"Act as my chief of staff. I have a one-to-one with /[Person] this morning. Identify the key issues we need to discuss and any outstanding email or chat threads between us that still need a reply."
Task Lists (Daily & Project)

Two flavours: a personal daily list ranked by urgency and importance, and a project list ordered by deadlines and dependencies.

Daily

"Create a daily task list prioritising urgent and important items."

Project

"Create a prioritised task list for [project] based on deadlines and dependencies."
